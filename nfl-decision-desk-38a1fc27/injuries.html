How depth and deficiency are measured

  1. Who is a starter. A player's snap share in his own unit (offence or defence) across the season to date. Share ≥ 0.50 = starter; the remaining players at his position group are ranked 2nd, 3rd, 4th string.
  2. What an absence costs. Replacement drop = availability weight × (the player's share − the next available player's share at the same position group). Summed by unit, it is the team's deficiency in percentage points of unit snaps.
  3. Availability weights. Out 1.0, Doubtful 0.75, Questionable 0.5; where the game status is not yet published, DNP 0.5, Limited 0.25, Full 0.0. These are documented assumptions (F14), not official probabilities.
  4. Excluded. “Not injury related” absences (rest, personal) are excluded from the deficiency score.

What this is not

This is not an official depth chart. The league does not publish one in machine-readable form (F05/F16). The method above is measurable, reproducible and published so you can judge it.

Does lineup loss actually matter?

Yes for margin, about half of it already priced. Controlling for prior scoring margin, a percentage point of relative deficiency is associated with -0.0391315 points of margin (n = 887, p = 6.07152e-05). The market line moves -0.0190095 points per point — about 49% of the effect — leaving a residual of -0.020122 points per point (p = 0.0242031).

Is there a bet in it?

Fading the more-injured team wins 51.4% ATS over 782 bets (95% CI 0.479053–0.548942), p = 1 against the 52.38% break-even. The ≥50 pp subset, fixed on 2023-2024 and tested on 2025-2026, wins 49.0% over 104 bets (p = 1). A starting quarterback out on exactly one side: 53.2% over 94 games.

Reading: none of these clear a Bonferroni threshold for a betting edge, and the effects are small enough that a stale line could explain them. The correct use of the metric is inside our own price (see own lines), not as a trigger.
