Q1. What would be considered a reasonable and normal rental increase in San Francisco — Outer Sunset 94122 — under local, state, and federal law?
Local (SF): for these continuing covered tenancies — 1.6% for increases effective 2026-03-01 → 2027-02-28, once per 12 months, base rent only — plus any properly banked years (additive, uncompounded); anything more needs a Rent Board petition (capital improvements / operating & maintenance certification) or a vacancy. SF Rent Board — annual increase announced ↗ · Learn about rent increases ↗
State (CA): AB 1482 caps most other tenancies at the lesser of 5% + CPI or 10% (SF-Oakland-Hayward CPI-U April 2026 = 3.8% → 8.8% max for 2026-08-01→2027-07-31). It is displaced here by SF’s stricter cap (§1947.12(d)(3)). Serving a >10% combined increase requires a 90-day notice (§827). CC §1947.12 ↗ · CC §827 ↗ · BLS CPI-SF (June 2026) ↗
Federal: no rent cap exists at the federal level; federal law contributes anti-discrimination rules only (Fair Housing Act). HUD ↗
Numbers for the two units: Unit 1 +$63.92 (+1.6%) → $4,058.92; Unit 2 +$50.40 (+1.6%) → $3,200.40; Unit 2 with full banking up to +$412.65 → $3,562.65 (F-02). Full math on the Unit 1/Unit 2 page.
Q2. How do big real estate corporations handle rental increases? How do individual SF property owners handle them?
Corporations: largely via revenue-management algorithms and shared market data. DOJ and 10 state AGs (incl. California) sued RealPage and six large landlords (Greystar, LivCor, Camden, Cushman/Pinnacle, Willow Bridge, Cortland; 1.3M+ units) alleging coordinated increases and suppressed competition/concessions via non-public shared data (Sherman Act); Cortland entered a proposed consent decree; the matter is in litigation and RealPage contests it. DOJ press release 25-21 (2025-01-07) ↗
Individual owners: same legal ceiling — the owner size is irrelevant under SF/CA law. Individual owners typically (a) impose the annual CPI increase, (b) skip and bank, or (c) petition for certified passthroughs on big-ticket work. They must equally hold a Housing Inventory license before annual/banked increases take effect, or the increase is null (§37.15). sf.gov ↗ (See Corporate vs individual for the full comparison and its limits — flag F-12.)
Q3. What is the correct way to inform tenants about a rental increase — and what are reasonable reasons?
Correct way: (1) verify coverage & obtain/confirm the rent increase license; (2) compute on current base rent only, exact 1/100-dollar amounts, never rounded up; (3) serve a written notice with the dollar amount, the percentage, the effective date, and the banked portion if any; (4) deliver 30 days ahead (combined ≤10% in 12 months) or 90 days ahead (>10%), plus 5 days if mailed; (5) keep proof of service. Reasonable reasons = the statutory ones: annual allowable %, properly banked years, certified capital-improvement or operating & maintenance passthroughs (petition first; passthroughs never become base rent and stop at the end of the amortization). Full checklist & template ↗ · sf.gov ↗ · CC §827 ↗
Q4. Will any increase pass review by a tenant-friendly advocate or lawyer (strict tenant protections; free tenant lawyer services)?
A lawful, uniform, fully documented increase — current license, correct math, proper notice, clean timing — is what tenant-side lawyers treat as normal business practice. They will reject: cap breaches, no license, wrong math, short notice, retaliation timing (180-day presumption, CC §1942.5 ↗), discrimination, or sham petitions. Nothing on this page “passes review” by itself; passing requires executing the checklist with the actual lease file and having a counselor/attorney sign off. Free help exists: SF Rent Board counselor 415-252-4600; Eviction Defense Collaborative 415-659-9184; Bay Area Legal Aid 415-982-1300. sf.gov — evictions & free help ↗
Q5. Would it be OK to raise only one unit’s rent, or is it safer to raise both?
Legal to raise one (within caps, documented, neutral reason, clean timing), but it invites comparative scrutiny: tenants will ask why them; lawyers will test for protected-class correlation, source-of-income targeting, or retaliation. The lowest-risk lawful pattern is the same formula applied to both units (each unit’s own anniversary and banked balance — which legitimately differ). “Raise both by the same dollar amount” is not lawful here (it would exceed Unit 1’s cap); “raise both by each unit’s own lawful maximum” is. Full analysis: Fair housing / one vs both ↗.
Q6. Would raising just Unit 1 (or just Unit 2) create discrimination liability?
Discrimination liability arises from the reason (protected class / source of income / familial status / disability / retaliation), not from the act of raising one unit. Risks: FHA (race, color, national origin, religion, sex, familial status, disability — terms/conditions of rental), FEHA (adds source of income, gender identity/expression, orientation, marital & veteran status, etc.), Unruh Act, and CC §1942.5’s 180-day presumption after a tenant exercises rights. Uniform formula treatment kills the comparison argument. HUD ↗ · CRD Fair Housing Fact Sheet ↗
Q7. Would it “make more sense” to raise both units to avoid discrimination lawsuits?
“Makes more sense” is right in risk terms, not in legal compulsion: raising both by their respective lawful maxima is the same action priced by the same formula — there is no “discrimination-safe” number that ignores the caps. Uniform method, uniform wording, unit-specific arithmetic, perfect paperwork. Details & decision table: Fair housing ↗. Also note Unit 1’s first-anniversary (2026-02-01, 1.4%) was skipped: serving it late moves the anniversary and forfeits the amount — a pure loss with no benefit (math ↗).
Q8. Are the units definitely rent controlled? Could they be exempt (new construction / single-family / condos / owner-occupied duplex)?
A 1940 two-unit building is the paradigm covered case; post-June-13-1979 construction, substantial rehabilitation, subsidized units, and single-family homes/condos (tenancy ≥ 1996) are the usual exemptions — an in-law/ADU makes it a two-unit building, not SFH. AB 1482 additionally exempts owner-occupied duplexes from the state cap only — SF’s cap still applies. Informal facility: only the building’s permit/certificate of occupancy records + Rent Board counselor can certify coverage — flag F-01. sf.gov coverage ↗ · Costa-Hawkins guidance ↗
